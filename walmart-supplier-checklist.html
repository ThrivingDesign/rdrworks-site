What happens after Walmart says yes: a supplier’s checklist

By Jason Rider · November 2026

The buyer liked your product. You got the yes. Most founders think that’s the finish line. It’s actually the start of a few weeks of paperwork, systems and specs, and the mistakes you make here show up later as deductions on your payments.

I took my own patented product into about 1,000 Walmart stores, mostly by myself. This is the checklist I wish someone had handed me. It’s written for Walmart, but Lowe’s, Tractor Supply and the other big chains work the same way in broad strokes. The system names change; the shape of the work doesn’t.

1. Read the supplier agreement before you sign it

The agreement sets the terms you’ll live with for years. Most of the money questions are decided here, not in the buyer meeting. Look for:

  • Allowances. Percentages taken off every invoice, for things like warehousing and defective or returned goods. Add them up. A few points off the top changes your margin.
  • Payment terms and early-pay discounts. How long until you’re paid, and what it costs to be paid sooner.
  • Returns. Who pays when product comes back, and how the retailer calculates the charge.
  • Freight terms. Whether the retailer picks up from you (collect) or you deliver (prepaid). This decides who arranges trucks and who pays for them.

If you priced your product before you knew these numbers, price it again now.

2. Get your barcodes right

You need GS1-issued barcodes, not cheap resold ones. Each selling unit gets its own number, and every case and inner pack gets its own code too, built as a hierarchy so the systems know how many units are in a case. Errors here are hard to undo once items are set up, so check the hierarchy carefully before you enter it.

3. Set up your items

Item setup happens in Walmart’s supplier portal. You’ll enter descriptions, dimensions, weights, case packs, cost, images and a long list of attributes. Measure real product in real packaging. Wrong dimensions or weights cause problems at the distribution center and can trigger chargebacks.

4. Check packaging against the retailer’s specs

The retailer has rules for shipping cases: label placement, barcode size and quality, case strength, and how product sits on a pallet. Your consumer packaging also needs the required text and a barcode that scans. Get the spec documents, compare them line by line with your actual cartons, and fix problems before your printer runs thousands of them.

5. Set up EDI

Big retailers send purchase orders and receive your shipping notices and invoices electronically, through EDI. Small suppliers usually use a web-based EDI provider rather than building anything. Compare monthly and per-document fees against how much you’ll actually ship. The core documents are the purchase order, the advance ship notice and the invoice, and each one has to match the others.

6. Learn the routing guide

The routing guide tells you how to request a pickup or book a delivery, how far ahead, and what happens if you miss a window. Read it all. Missed pickups, late shipments and short shipments carry fines, and the retailer tracks on-time, in-full performance closely.

7. Ship the first order

  • Confirm the purchase order matches what you can actually ship, in quantity and date.
  • Request the pickup or appointment as the routing guide says, early.
  • Label every case and pallet to spec.
  • Prepare the bill of lading and send the advance ship notice so it matches what’s on the truck.
  • Keep copies of everything, including signed paperwork and photos of loaded pallets.

8. Read your first payment line by line

Your first remittance will likely show deductions: allowances, maybe fines, maybe chargebacks you didn’t expect. Match each one to the agreement and to your records. Some will be legitimate. Some won’t, and you can dispute those within a set window. Small brands often leave money on the table here simply because nobody looks.

Other things you’ll be asked for

  • A certificate of insurance at the coverage levels the retailer requires
  • Product testing or compliance documents, depending on the category
  • Banking and tax forms for vendor setup
  • A supplier account in the retailer’s reporting system, where you’ll watch sales and store inventory

The short version

None of this is hard once you’ve done it. All of it is confusing the first time, and the costs of getting it wrong arrive later, quietly, as deductions. Take it in order: agreement, barcodes, items, packaging, EDI, routing, first shipment, first payment.
