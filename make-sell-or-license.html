Make it, sell it, or license it? I’ve done all three

By Jason Rider · November 2026

Every inventor with a patent eventually asks the same question: do I make this myself, sell it into retail, or license it to someone who already does both? Most people answer it once, early, and live with the result. I’ve had to answer it three times with the same product, and each answer taught me something the others couldn’t.

The product is C-BITE, a clip that connects garden stakes so you can build plant supports of any size and shape. I prototyped it on a 3D printer, and after many versions it was patented in December 2019 with five independent claims. The clips connect to each other in more than 140 ways, and that turned out to matter as much for the business as for the garden.

I made it on my own molding machines. I sold it into about 1,000 Walmart stores, plus Lowe’s, Tractor Supply, QVC and Amazon. And now I’m moving it to a license. Here’s what each door gave me, what each one cost, and how I’d choose if I were starting over.

Door one: make it yourself

I bought two used injection-molding presses outright, small 35-ton machines, and ran one mold in one material, acetal. It was a lean setup on purpose: no tooling shop and no resin changes, just one part made well.

What it gave me. Control. I could run clips when I needed them, check every batch, and print “Made in USA” on the bag because it was true. My cost per part was low because I wasn’t paying anyone else’s margin. When a retailer asked whether I could supply, I knew the answer without calling a factory.

What it cost. Floor space, power, and attention. A factory, even a two-machine one, needs a building, and the building needs rent whether the presses run or not. Every dollar of inventory sitting on pallets was a dollar not doing anything else. And I became a plant manager on top of being the inventor, the salesman and the shipping department.

The biggest lesson: the mold is the asset; the machines are replaceable with cash. When it came time to downsize, I worked out that disconnecting, moving, storing and recommissioning the presses would cost roughly what it would take to sell them and buy others later. Machines are a commodity. A good mold for a patented part is not. If you’re deciding whether to make your own product, own the tooling, and think hard before you own the iron. A contract molder can run your mold while you sell.

Door two: sell it into retail

C-BITE made it to Walmart, Lowe’s, Tractor Supply, QVC and Amazon, with Walmart the biggest at about 1,000 stores. For a one-person company, that’s a lot of shelf.

What it gave me. Scale and proof. Nothing tells the world a product works like a big-box shelf. It also gave me something I didn’t value enough at the time: sales history. Real numbers from real stores became the most useful thing I had when I started talking about licensing.

What it cost. More than the retailer’s margin. Allowances come off every invoice. Payment terms are long, and getting paid early costs a discount. Deductions show up for routing misses, labeling errors and returns, and you pay for the returns. On top of that you need barcodes, EDI, packaging specs and a routing guide to follow. I was the operations department for most of that run, and some days I was the person unloading the truck.

Two things surprised me:

  • You don’t own the relationship, the buyer does, and buyers change. Over the years I worked with a series of buyers and coverage people. An agency that helped me early was acquired and outgrew my account. Each change can reset how your product is seen, and decisions about your store count are theirs to make.
  • A durable product sells once. C-BITEs are reusable for many seasons, which is great for gardeners and hard on repeat sales. If your product doesn’t need replacing, retail growth has to come from more stores and more products, not more purchases per customer.

The lesson: price for the whole waterfall from day one. Work backward from the shelf price through the retailer’s margin, allowances, deductions and freight before you quote a buyer anything. Door three: license it

I’m now moving C-BITE to a license, where another company makes and sells it and pays me a royalty. It isn’t a retreat. It’s what the first two doors made possible.

What it gives. Income without the working capital. The licensee carries the inventory, the warehouse, the trucks and the buyer relationships. A company that already sells into my category can put the product on more shelves than I could alone, and I get my time back for the next idea.

What it costs. Control and margin. A royalty is a small slice of each sale compared with selling direct, and the licensee decides how the product is priced, packaged and pushed. You also need the right licensee: one that already sells to the buyers you want, in your category, and has a reason to care about your product rather than just shelve it.

What makes it possible. Leverage. A patent alone gets you a meeting. A patent plus sales history, proven manufacturing and a product buyers have already said yes to gets you a deal. Everything I learned making and selling C-BITE is now part of the pitch.

One honest note: licensing deals move slowly. Plan your cash as if the first royalty check arrives later than anyone promises, because it will.

How to choose

The three doors aren’t ranked. They suit different products, different bank accounts and different people.

The questions that decide it:

  1. Do you want to run a business, or own an invention? Making and selling mean running a company. Licensing means letting someone else run it.
  2. How long can you wait to get paid? Retail pays late, and licensing pays later still at the start.
  3. Does your product get bought again? Consumables reward the retail grind. Durable goods need more stores or more products to grow.
  4. Is there already a company selling to your buyer? If yes, they may be your licensee, or your competition.
  5. What evidence do you have? A patent opens doors. Sales history walks you through them.

What I’d tell myself at the start

  • Decide which door you’re building toward. Each one wants different evidence. A licensee wants sales history and a clean patent; a retailer wants supply you can prove and a price that works for them.
  • Own the mold, rent the machines unless your volume truly needs your own presses.
  • Price from the shelf down, not from your cost up. Include allowances, deductions and freight before the first quote.
  • Keep a deductions reserve and read every payment line by line.
  • Build more than one buyer relationship. Buyers move, and your account moves with them.
  • Treat every sale as part of a future pitch. Even if you never license, good records make every next conversation easier.

None of these doors is the wrong one. The mistake is walking through one without knowing what it costs. I’ve paid for all three, and I’d rather you didn’t have to.
